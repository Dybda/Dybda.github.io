About

Welcome to my academic profile. I am a PhD Researcher at the European University Institute, supervised by Prof. Herman van de Werfhorst and Prof. Simon Hix. My dissertation asks how higher education shapes political behaviour and orientation, and I work more broadly on political communication and the media environment.

Methodologically, I combine quasi-experimental causal inference with computational text analysis. On the design side that means difference-in-differences and its recent variants, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, interrupted time series, and distributional and heterogeneous treatment effects. On the measurement side I build text-as-data pipelines — ensembles of large language models to classify course and media content, sentence embeddings, and embedding regression — and link panel surveys to administrative records. Most of my work is explicitly comparative, spanning Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Denmark and Greenland.

Substantively, this ranges from how coursework cultivates civic engagement and left-libertarian orientations, to how electoral systems convert those dispositions into votes for Greens or Labour, to how a foreign territorial claim reshaped postcolonial media coverage of Greenland in the Danish press.

Alongside research, I led the Danish team for EU&I, a voting advice application that reached 1.2 million voters, and I hold the EUI Certificate in Teaching and Learning in Higher Education. Earlier collaborative work covered EU regulatory governance (published in Regulation & Governance) and the governance of migration.

I am currently on my second research visit of my PhD, this time in Berlin at the DIW (German Institute for Economic Research), where I am working on my dissertation. Feel free to explore my current projects, past work, and future research directions using the navigation above.

Ongoing Projects

Content, Not Selection: Course Exposure, Political Orientation, and Civic Engagement in College

Does college shape students' politics, or do politically distinctive students simply choose similar courses? I separate content from selection by measuring exposure not by field but by course content — scoring the communicative and cultural content of 6,635 course descriptions with an ensemble of large language models — and by exploiting a general-education requirement that compels coursework students cannot sort on. Using panel and administrative data from a large public university, I find that this content substantially raises civic engagement (0.3 SD) while shifting political orientations only modestly leftward (0.11 SD). Coursework cultivates engaged citizens more than partisans. [Panel / GE requirement / LLM ensemble]

Who Changes in College? Selection, and the Political Returns to Higher Education

Does higher education cause political change, or do graduates' choices reflect who was always going to enroll? This paper reframes the question as one of political returns, distinguishing engagement from partisan direction and asking how returns are distributed across the contrasting party systems of Germany and the United Kingdom. Attainment shifts graduates toward socially liberal and left-libertarian parties and away from the radical right; party systems, not education systems, govern how this disposition reaches the ballot. These returns are unevenly distributed — concentrating among the most likely to attend in Germany and the least likely in the UK. [Distributional / heterogeneous treatment effects]

Beyond the Major: How Exposure to Social Sciences and Humanities Shapes Political Information Behavior

This paper examines whether course-level exposure to social sciences and humanities causally shifts students' political news consumption, using longitudinal data linked to administrative records to separate selection from treatment. Outcomes focus on political media preferences and information-seeking behaviour. [Stacked DiD]

Speaking for Greenland: Trump's Territorial Claim and the Sourcing of Danish Solidarity

Donald Trump's stated intentions for Greenland made the Danish Realm newsworthy in a way it had not been for decades. We analyse Danish coverage of Greenland from January 2019 to February 2026 — 6,579 articles from a fixed panel of eight outlets for volume, and 2,776 full-text articles for framing — using interrupted time series and regression discontinuity analyses of sentence-embedding measures to estimate the volume of coverage and the standing it affords Greenlandic actors. Coverage increased fifteenfold after Trump's December 2024 claim, while Greenlandic voices became less present and Danish standing grew from a quarter to more than a third of the coverage. [Interrupted time series / RDD / sentence embeddings]

Future

Research Directions

I'm interested in pursuing projects that leverage large-N observational data to draw conclusions about behaviours, particularly in terms of exploring differences in skills/education and political values and engagement.

Planned Studies

  • Sweden's higher-educated peers and network spillover influences on political outcomes
  • Field norms in the classroom: a lab experiment at a business school and a sociology programme in France, testing whether students' budget choices respond to seeing their peers' real choices and to having to defend their own in front of the room. Preregistration under embargo.
